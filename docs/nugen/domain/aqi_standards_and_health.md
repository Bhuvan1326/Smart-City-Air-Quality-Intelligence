# Air Quality Standards, Pollutants, and Health Guidance (India)

## AQI bands (CPCB / NAAQS convention)

India's Central Pollution Control Board (CPCB) classifies the Air Quality
Index into six bands. When interpreting an AQI value in this platform, map it
to these bands and describe health meaning accordingly:

- 0–50 Good: minimal impact; air quality is acceptable for everyone.
- 51–100 Satisfactory: acceptable; unusually sensitive people should watch
  for minor breathing discomfort on prolonged exertion.
- 101–200 Moderate: breathing discomfort for people with asthma, children,
  and the elderly; reduce prolonged outdoor exertion for sensitive groups.
- 201–300 Poor: breathing discomfort for most people on prolonged exposure;
  children, elderly, and people with heart or lung disease should avoid
  outdoor exertion.
- 301–400 Very Poor: respiratory illness on prolonged exposure; everyone
  should limit outdoor activity, sensitive groups stay indoors.
- 401–500 Severe: affects healthy people and seriously impacts those with
  existing disease; avoid outdoor activity entirely, keep windows closed.

The sub-index is driven by the worst pollutant, so PM2.5 typically dominates
the AQI in Indian cities. Always state which pollutant drives the reading
when that information is available.

## Pollutant reference

PM2.5 (particulate matter ≤2.5 µm): penetrates deep into the lungs and enters
the bloodstream. Primary sources: vehicle exhaust, construction dust, waste
burning, industrial combustion, secondary aerosol formation. NAAQS 24-hour
standard: 60 µg/m³; annual: 40 µg/m³. WHO 24-hour guideline: 15 µg/m³.
Chronic exposure is linked to cardiovascular disease, reduced lung function,
and aggravated asthma.

PM10 (≤10 µm): coarse fraction — road dust, construction, soil resuspension.
NAAQS 24-hour: 100 µg/m³; annual: 60 µg/m³. Irritates airways and eyes.

NO2 (nitrogen dioxide): traffic and combustion indicator. NAAQS 24-hour:
80 µg/m³. Worsens asthma, increases airway inflammation, contributes to
secondary PM and ozone formation.

SO2 (sulfur dioxide): industrial fuel burning (brick kilns, power plants,
diesel with high sulfur). NAAQS 24-hour: 80 µg/m³. Triggers bronchospasm in
asthmatics; precursor of sulfate particulates.

CO (carbon monoxide): incomplete combustion — vehicles, biomass burning.
NAAQS 8-hour: 2 mg/m³; 1-hour: 4 mg/m³. Binds hemoglobin, reduces oxygen
delivery; dangerous for people with heart disease and during pregnancy.
Report CO in mg/m³, not µg/m³.

O3 (ozone): secondary pollutant formed by NOx + VOCs in sunlight. NAAQS
8-hour: 100 µg/m³. Peaks in the afternoon; causes coughing, throat
irritation, reduced lung function during exercise.

## Health guidance by audience

Citizens: on Poor days and above, prefer indoor exercise, use N95/KN95 masks
outdoors, keep windows closed during peak traffic hours, run air purifiers
if available, and check the forecast before planning outdoor activity.
Children and elderly should avoid morning walks on Very Poor/Severe days —
early-morning temperature inversions trap pollutants near the ground.

Officers and inspectors: prioritise enforcement on days forecast Poor or
worse. Construction-dust complaints spike when PM10 rises without a matching
NO2 rise; waste-burning reports correlate with evening PM2.5 spikes plus CO.
Brick-kiln and industrial clusters drive localised SO2 excursions.

Administrators: sustained Moderate+ periods warrant public-health advisories,
construction activity restrictions, and traffic management in the affected
wards. Track weekly ward-level AQI to evaluate interventions.

## Interpreting trends

Diurnal pattern: AQI is typically worst in the early morning (inversion) and
evening rush hour, and best in the afternoon when the boundary layer grows.
Weekday-vs-weekend differences mainly reflect traffic. Seasonal: post-monsoon
and winter months (October–January) are worst in inland cities like Pune due
to low winds, low mixing height, and agricultural burning upwind.

Weather interaction: high humidity raises apparent particle mass
(hygroscopic growth); rain scavenges PM; strong winds disperse pollutants.
Always label forecast values as Predicted and sensor readings as Observed —
never blend the two without a label.
